Dear Business Leaders, Think Again

November 2026 / Translated from Slovenian / Hang the Doggy

Slovenian original: Dragi gospodarstveniki, razmislite še enkrat

The Slovenian text is the original. This English version was translated with the help of AI.

Dear business leaders, this text is addressed to you. Because I value you and agree that Slovenia needs you. Without a successful economy there is not, and cannot be, a successful society. Much troubles me in the heated public debate ahead of the December referendum on the emergency omnibus law. I find it inappropriate and unproductive that the debate is increasingly turning into a dogmatic, ideological class struggle between those who have less and those of you who have more.

Unlike some, I have no difficulty at all living in a society that also includes wealthy people who built their wealth through their own knowledge and work. After all, the Slovenian constitution guarantees the right to private property and free economic initiative. Dear business leaders, Slovenia needs you. I agree with you that the appropriate level of the tax burden, the efficiency of the public sector and, above all, the long-term competitiveness of the Slovenian economy are legitimate questions to which we must find the right answers.

I follow with interest the explanations of the possible positive effects of some parts of the emergency law. I do not claim categorically that it will be harmful or unjustified in every respect. The problem is that the law was adopted in this politically and legally highly questionable omnibus form. True, the previous government committed the same sin when it adopted the Šutar Act, but pointing to past mistakes cannot be an excuse for repeating them. Whatever positive effects individual parts of the omnibus law may have, they pale beside its financial consequences, which will inevitably and painfully cut into the social contract on which Slovenia has built its democracy and economic development since independence. And that should worry you too.

My argument is that bringing into force the Act on the Development of Slovenia, on which we will decide in the December referendum, would in the long run harm you as well, whatever the joy at your next tax assessment or two. Let me explain why. First, because the story that the law will benefit all citizens does not hold water. I am sure you know this yourselves, because you are smart and capable. I say this without a trace of sarcasm: Slovenia can be truly proud of its successful business people. But let us be honest. The only guaranteed financial consequence of the law will be a deep hole in the budget. The promised crumbs that politics throws to the plebs stand on very shaky legs. The VAT cut on some foods will most likely not end up in citizens' pockets but will instead raise retailers' margins, as happened in Croatia. The same will happen with the tax breaks for renting out flats. Why would landlords, in a market where demand far exceeds supply, pass the extra profit on to tenants? Out of a sense of social solidarity? Solidarity is not only a noble value but the foundation of a successful, development-oriented society. And because it is so important, it must be enforced systemically, not left to the goodwill of individuals.

In the introduction I said that I am troubled by the ideological and dogmatic arguments and clichés in this campaign. But they do not come only from the law's opponents. Let us be fair: the government's numbers simply do not add up. Not only because of the considerable differences between the projected deficit figures the government sends to Brussels, those it announces at home, and those calculated by the Fiscal Council, but also because the government's explanations of how this hole will be filled are very vague, unconvincing and unserious. A substantial cut in funding for social transfers will be covered by uncovering fraud? Significantly less money for healthcare will have no consequences for medical care, because work in health institutions will become more efficient? Esteemed business leaders, I ask you sincerely: would you be prepared to build your business plan and embark on new investments on the basis of such non-credible assurances about financial conditions that matter to your operations?

Second, because I not only believe in, but agree with, your insistence on the importance of the competitiveness of the Slovenian economy. At the same time, I am convinced that you cannot and do not want to build this competitiveness merely on cutting labour costs, even below the level that ensures a decent living, but above all on knowledge. In the high-tech world we live in, this will be the decisive criterion determining who will be successful and competitive in the future, and who will become the economic doormat of foreign capital. So I ask you again: from this point of view, how do you see the measures the government is announcing in preparation for the consequences of the emergency law, should it come into force? The drastic 60 percent cut in funding for basic and applied research projects announced by the Ministry of Education, Science and Youth? Or the radical reduction of more than a quarter of a billion euros in funding for primary, secondary and higher education?

Third, because I agree with you that a successful economy is a precondition for a successful society and a welfare state. But the reverse is also true. Not only because the Slovenian constitution also states that Slovenia is a social state, but because the sustainable, long-term success of the economy does not depend solely on your abilities, knowledge and work. It also depends on good-quality infrastructure, a functioning and efficient public sector, social and political stability, a healthcare system that takes care of everyone, because healthy people work better than sick ones, investment in knowledge, pluralism that fosters innovation and enables democratic oversight of the executive, and legal certainty and predictability, which only a consistent and effective rule of law can provide.

The state needs a successful economy, but economic success and development cannot be measured only by how much tax you will pay. This does not mean that the level and distribution of the tax burden are not important questions we must discuss and agree on. But what also matters is what you get for that tax and what you urgently need in order to remain successful. So think carefully about what could happen to you, too, if the welfare state collapses. Let us set aside that we wrote it into the constitution. Let us also forget for a moment that social solidarity, which leaves no one in the lurch, is a moral value, although I believe that you too care how people who have less than you are faring. So I ask you something very simple: do you really want to live in a society seething with anger at social injustice? One in which people take to the streets because they cannot see a doctor, have no roof over their heads and can barely, if at all, support their families? One in which young people protest because the state doesn't give a damn about their future, as is happening across much of Europe? Do you really think this will not affect you? That you will go on being as successful as you are?

And finally, because in this story you have ended up in cahoots with a politics that doesn't give a damn about your future development and success either. In exchange for tax breaks, what it expects from you above all is support for a permanent seizure of power and for loosening the democratic foundations of society, including the rule of law, which could hit you very hard in the future. On the head. And in the pocket. If you think this is only a theoretical threat, consider the governing coalition's latest proposal, which, following the recipe of foreign authoritarians, seeks to prevent civil society from functioning, civil society being, alongside an independent judiciary and media, one of the key mechanisms of oversight of the executive. And the announcement, even if temporarily withdrawn, that the National Security Council would be turned into the prime minister's defence circle. Why should this worry you? Look at how your colleagues fared in Hungary under Orbán, in Serbia under Vučić, not to mention Putin's Russia. Their profits depended less on the tax rate than on how much they had to pay the master for protection.

Dear business leaders, I want to believe that your commitment to the well-being of all citizens is sincere, but the path you are supporting is not the right one. A reform that is so obviously socially unbalanced, to put it mildly, is doomed to fail. Instead of the predictability you need, there will be political quarrels, social tensions and changes as soon as someone else is in power. The costs of instability will most likely be higher for you than the money the tax breaks would have brought you.

What will happen in this nasty and unpredictable world is hard to predict. But the fact is that more and more people realise that things cannot go on like this. Little tales of prosperity that is about to start trickling down any moment now no longer convince anyone. You too bear responsibility for overcoming the social polarisation you complain about. There is still time. Because I sincerely value you and believe that Slovenia needs your abilities, knowledge and success, I kindly ask you: think again. Not only for the sake of the people who have little, but also for the sake of you who have more. In the end, it will be better for everyone.
