The Politician Without a Lid

November 2026 / Translated from Slovenian / Hang the Doggy

Slovenian original: Politik brez pokrovčka

The Slovenian text is the original. This English version was translated with the help of AI.

The British comedian Jake Lambert, who is also known as the joke machine, has an excellent series of short videos in which, as a historian from the future, he explains how people lived in 2026. “In most homes we found containers called Tupperware. We assume they were originally intended for storing leftover food. Surprisingly, however, people kept them in their homes for years and decades, even though not a single container had a lid of the right size.”

I can imagine how the political scientists (or comedians) of the future will try to work out why, for years and decades after independence, Slovenian politics was held in the grip of a single political personality, even though—you know—containers and lids …

Make no mistake: over this long, very, very long period, Janez Janša has shown that he has a well-developed instinct for political survival; otherwise he would not have stayed at the centre of Slovenian politics for so many years. But instinct does not necessarily mean the political genius we are so fond of attributing to him. His longevity is to a large extent a projection of the weaknesses of his rivals and a consequence of a weak political culture which, in thirty-five years, we have not managed to raise to a level that would allow democracy to function even approximately normally. On the contrary: political culture has lately been going downhill ever more steeply; otherwise we would not have to deal with characters such as the current Speaker of the National Assembly and his company of village and town eccentrics.

If we manage, even for a moment, to wriggle mentally out of the stuffy bubble of the Slovenian political scene, we really cannot understand why, almost since the first free elections, we have been trapped in a political story directed by someone with the charisma and rhetorical skills of a funeral director. A retired one.

Everyone—his supporters and opponents, journalists and political analysts—speaks of Janša as a master of four-dimensional chess who is always a few moves ahead of the competition. In reality, Janša is to a large extent not a self-made man: we created him and we keep him going ourselves. He may get on our nerves, yet it seems that without him we neither know how nor are able to imagine Slovenian politics and the state.

As I said, Janša is skilful, there is no doubt about that. Two traits above all have helped his political longevity: a paranoid distrust, with which, even in times of political slip-ups (and there have been quite a few), he has prevented any internal criticism and competition; and an absence of ethical brakes, which makes him prepared to do (almost) anything to stay in power.

That, however, would not have been enough had he not faced—with rare exceptions—such pitiful and powerless competition. The disappointment, weariness and resistance of the electorate towards the endless spiral of ever new non-Janšas, who swing into power after each of his terms convinced that they are there thanks to their own political genius, and behave accordingly, make Janša’s longevity possible and at the same time open the door to models à la Stevanović. Which is surprising, because Janša, despite his aura of infallibility and invincibility, makes mistakes. Because he cannot change who he is. Because under pressure he flips his lid every time. And a politician who cannot control himself makes mistakes.

In his political career so far, Janša has not shown many original ideas. What he does, as a rule, is cut-and-paste imitation of the populist culture war and of neoliberal cons, which he imposes, unprocessed, here at home and on us. There are many examples; let me mention only the most topical one, on which we will decide in the December referendum. The Slovenia Development Act, which was drafted by his coalition partners but which Janša adopted and turned into the key test of his government, is strongly reminiscent of the One Big Beautiful Bill that the Republicans passed on Trump’s orders a little over a year ago. Tax breaks for the wealthiest, and in return a few crumbs for the masses, who are supposed to be so thrilled at the promise that they will (perhaps) have a euro or a dollar more left over each month that they are prepared to overlook deep cuts to their social security. Trump hoped this law would win him the latest US congressional elections. The results showed that in America, too, ever fewer people believe that the money you send upwards will one day start trickling down. How many such naive people there are in Slovenia, we will see in December.

Government representatives and experts agree that the Development Act will create a hole in the budget; they differ only in their estimates of how deep it will be. Even if we believe the government’s figures, it will have to be filled. You may well believe that the money will trickle in from somewhere—the government is not exactly precise on this point—but it is more likely that we will once again be forced to rebalance the public finances. I hope we have not forgotten how Janša goes about that. The welfare state, which is also enshrined in our constitution and which already stands on shaky legs, cannot survive another ZUJF (the sweeping austerity law of 2012, passed under Janša’s government). And when there is no welfare state, there will be no democracy either.

But Janša does not use his cronies from the populist international only as inspiration for a sub-Alpine version of undermining liberal democracy; he also receives concrete help from them. Orbán, Netanyahu, Vučić. Takeovers of independent media, funding for party mouthpieces, interference in Slovenian politics and elections. Of course, these gentlemen give nothing for free. That is why Janša again and again finds himself in a position where, because of his debts to foreign backers, he has to do things that objectively harm him in the Slovenian and European political space. Submissive, unconditional and morally reprehensible support for Netanyahu’s policies. Congratulations to Trump. Standing by Orbán to the bitter end. Support for the Polish President Nawrocki in his dispute with Prime Minister Tusk, former President of the European Council and of the European People’s Party, to which—at least nominally and for now—the SDS also belongs. A hysterical reaction to Vučić’s defeat in the elections in Serbia, which put the fear of God into him in particular.

These are not arguments in favour of the thesis of an infallible and invincible politician. They point rather to someone who, out of a lust for power and his own incompetence and helplessness, has begun to trip himself up. In just a few months, Janša has lost almost all the key foreign supporters and patrons who helped him win and hold on to power. Instead of Orbán, Magyar; instead of Vučić, the students. In the US, humiliated Republicans and a weakened Trump. He still has Netanyahu, whose fate, however, is also uncertain. Whatever we think of his possible successor in the prime minister’s chair, he will certainly not have much understanding for Netanyahu’s little errand boy on the sunny side of the Alps.

Even if we may not yet notice it in the Slovenian bubble, Janša is nervous—and he has good reasons to be. He is flipping his lid. Even paranoiacs sometimes have good reason to worry.
