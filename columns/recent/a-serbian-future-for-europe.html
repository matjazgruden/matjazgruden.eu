A Serbian Future for Europe?

November 2026 / Delo / Remembering Democracy

Slovenian original: Srbska prihodnost za Evropo?

A lightly edited English version of the article first published in Slovenian in the daily Delo on 13 November 2026.

The outcome of the recent elections in Serbia and the fall of the Vučić regime seem to have surprised almost everybody, including the students themselves.

After the initial shock, congratulations started to arrive, some immediately, some after a few days’ delay. But arrive they did, from almost everybody. The exception was the Slovenian Prime Minister, who felt this was the right moment to threaten Serbia’s future leadership with a veto on its European aspirations unless they hand over the secret service files of a country that ceased to exist more than three decades ago, but seems to be doing very well in the Prime Minister’s head. A victory of young people over a populist would-be authoritarian was clearly, in every sense, too close to home.

Once everyone had recovered from the surprise, the first “questions” began to emerge. In polite diplomatese, a “question” usually means “I am not sure I am really comfortable with this.”

Some question the “European aspirations” of the student movement, the very same aspirations the Slovenian Prime Minister had already threatened to block.

Others are concerned about the ideological breadth of the victorious coalition. It includes not only the young and progressive students we have all suddenly decided to love, after more or less ignoring them for the better part of two years, but also a more conservative and nationalist faction. This has raised eyebrows, including among those who did not seem bothered by Vučić’s very prominent instrumentalisation of Serbian nationalism.

Finally, many are asking how a very broad and decentralised movement will be able to govern. It has taken every important decision after long discussions at student assemblies, or plenums, as they call them. That method of democratic decision-making seems ill-suited to the speed and efficiency expected of executive power.

None of these three questions is unfounded. The changes in Serbia have confronted us with something new. The coming weeks and months will certainly be challenging, and the long-term success and sustainability of this electoral victory are far from guaranteed.

At the same time, each of the three questions also opens prospects for new, innovative and perhaps more successful approaches to some of the most pressing and intractable problems democracies face in today’s messed-up world.

On relations with the European Union, the “questions” about the students’ future intentions build on earlier concerns about the absence of EU symbols at the protests. These were usually voiced with implied disapproval of these young people’s obvious lack of gratitude. Yet many EU representatives did not hesitate to jump on the students’ bandwagon as soon as Vučić conceded, and enthusiastically welcomed the outcome as a vote for Serbia’s European future. On social networks, such statements were often met with the customary Balkan directness, which I have decided not to quote, on the slim chance that a child might ever lay eyes on this text.

The surprise and disappointment that the students did not wrap themselves in blue and gold while bringing down the Vučić regime is yet another manifestation of the hypocrisy that is largely responsible for young Serbians’ lukewarm attitude towards European institutions.

A year and a half ago, their representatives cycled more than fifteen hundred kilometres to Brussels and Strasbourg, hoping to present their case to these very institutions. They were met with hesitation, embarrassment and an evident wish not to displease Vučić, who still seemed untouchable at the time. At the Council of Europe, the decision to receive them came only after long and painful back-and-forths, but in the end it happened. The meeting with these young people at the European Youth Centre, which I attended with the then Deputy Secretary General, will remain one of the most emotional and inspiring moments of my thirty-plus years in European institutions. But the fact remains: for many years, up to the very end, institutional Europe preferred the (illusion of) stability provided by Vučić to concrete support for the European values these young people were fighting for.

There is, however, a more optimistic reading of this situation, with potential benefits not just for Serbia but for the European Union as well. Even without European flags at the protests, the Serbian students’ demands were substantively identical to what the European Union expects of its future members: pluralist democracy, independent media, integrity and accountability, rule of law, human rights… But they were not driven by eagerness to please Brussels. They grew out of the students’ aspirations for their country, their society and their future. The message, however blunt (we are in the Balkans, after all), was “you can stick your wilted carrot where the sun doesn’t shine, we are doing this for ourselves.” Bluntness aside, that is a very positive message. It puts the incentives in the right place and could significantly improve the chances of successful and genuinely meaningful future accessions, not only for Serbia but for other candidate countries too.

There are, however, legitimate concerns about Serbia’s future relations with Russia and its attitude towards Kosovo. Even if we accept the logic of setting foreign policy conundrums aside at first, not least because of the ideological diversity within the movement, these questions cannot be postponed for long. Russia is not known for distinguishing consistently between foreign and domestic affairs. Any continuation of Vučić’s policy of sitting on two chairs would very quickly become an obstacle to genuine democratic change in Serbia.

Then there are the concerns about the diversity of ideological views within the victorious coalition. Fair enough, but I think they deserve deeper reflection. That the movement brings together very different opinions on key political and social challenges should not be a problem in itself. After all, its main objective was to end a regime in which people were coerced into thinking the same. This holds provided that, despite their differences, there is consensus on the most fundamental principles of a democratic society and the rule of law. That seems to be the case, as confirmed by Professor Radomir Saičić, one of the movement’s leaders from its conservative wing and a possible candidate for the upcoming presidential elections. If they are sincere and successful, this bodes well for democracy, and not only in Serbia. Once they have laid solid foundations for a genuinely democratic and pluralist society, they can quarrel about their differences until the cows come home. That works as long as they quarrel within the parameters of such a society, not about them, which is what is happening in most of the Western world today.

Finally, there is the question of whether a broad, decentralised movement of active and engaged citizens can turn itself into some form of political organisation capable of governing the state. Admittedly, this will be a challenge. But again, let us look at it from another perspective. For years, political scientists, democracy experts and activists have been searching for ways to counter democratic backsliding and make our democracies more resilient against populist and authoritarian threats. There have been plenty of ideas and relatively little practical evidence of progress, until now. Across this whole range of ideas and approaches there is one common thread. The solution will require stronger democratic participation, through new and innovative ways of including engaged citizens in democratic processes. Yes, just as we are seeing in Serbia today.

Serbia’s future is still uncertain, but what is happening there matters. If the students succeed, and I believe they can, it may reverberate across the continent and possibly beyond. In the future, we may talk not only about what the European perspective can bring to Serbia, but also, and even more, about what the Serbian experience can teach the rest of Europe and the world.
