Just Let Me Be Honest

5 December 2017 / Translated from Slovenian / Remembering Democracy

Slovenian original: Dovolite mi le, da sem iskren

The Slovenian text is the original. This English version was translated with the help of AI.

One cold February morning in 1999, I stood in an office on the ground floor of the Palais de l’Europe, as the seat of the Council of Europe is officially called, shifting nervously from foot to foot and wondering whether anyone would finally notice I was there. That was clearly not the first concern of the elderly bald gentleman in a blue suit, once surely elegant but now a little tired, who was slowly and deliberately arranging souvenirs on a shelf beside his desk. It was an imposing collection of the cheap institutional kitsch that is so often handed out on official occasions, especially in Europe’s East. When the last ceramic plate with a gilded three-headed eagle had found its place, the gentleman turned to me, gestured at the collection and asked: “So, what do you think?” For reasons I cannot explain, at the last moment I swallowed the answer that would have been far more fitting for a young bureaucrat and blurted out: “With all due respect, sir, may I be honest?” My words clearly surprised him. He sat down, studied me motionless for a few moments, and then cheerfully snapped: “Fuck off.” The man who had represented Scotland’s Inverness in the House of Commons for thirty-three years, newly minted member of the House of Lords Baron Russell-Johnston of Minginish in Highland, and freshly elected President of the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe, stood up, shook my hand and said: “Hello, I’m Russell. Would you like to work for me?”

For the next three years I was his political adviser, speechwriter, travelling companion and confidant. Together we travelled across half of Europe and a good part of the world, and met a whole heap of interesting people and an even bigger heap of less interesting ones. From Barack Obama, back when he was still a completely unknown young senator from Illinois, to Ibrahim Rugova, whom we had to reach by getting through Serbian police checkpoints; from committed, idealistic reformers of a newly reunited Europe to ruddy-cheeked former party officials who had selflessly shouldered the burden of steering political and economic change in many European countries. During a visit to Moscow and Chechnya in January 2000, Russell first gave Putin a dressing-down for being badly late to a meeting (which then went on for a full three hours), and then, on landing in Chechnya, with bullets whistling, turned down the camouflage uniform that the Russian interior minister offered him “for his own safety”. “Thank you very much indeed,” Russell said, “but I think they are aiming at you, not at me.” We shared many interesting, funny and also sad moments. I was with him at Warsaw airport when he got a call that one of his three sons had been badly injured in an accident and would probably not survive. There followed hours of anxious waiting, a few bottles of wine and a long monologue about life, family, love and chances missed forever. His son, fortunately, later recovered, while I suffered badly the next day from what Russell liked to call Scottish self-harm.

We were colleagues throughout his term as President, and friends until his death. Why a British politician who could have taught the art of oratory at a university chose a Slovenian as his speechwriter is still a mystery to me. All I know is that I learned an enormous amount from him. Russell was a convinced European who believed in Europe as a guarantee of peace and as a civilisational project founded on real democracy, human rights and solidarity. He was a liberal who believed in a free society that protects the rights and freedoms of the individual, respects human dignity and rests on social justice and humanism. He was in politics for his values, and perhaps that is precisely why, in a career of four decades, he never came to power. But as he put it: “If you lose power defending your values, you are left with your self-respect and the hope that you will succeed next time. But if you lose your values fighting for power, you are left with nothing.” Unlike most politicians, Russell did not know how to pretend. If he valued someone, he showed it; if he did not like someone, he said so; if someone bored him, he fell asleep. When Vojislav Koštunica came to power in Belgrade after the fall of Slobodan Milošević, Russell did not join the international applause. He wrote in the Herald Tribune that Koštunica’s “moderate nationalism” could bring Serbia not a future but merely a recycled version of its past. He supported Kosovo’s independence very early on, but that did not stop him from accusing NATO members, over their bombing with depleted uranium, that it was not only their uranium that was depleted, but also their ethics and morals. Lord Russell-Johnston saw politics as a responsibility and as an obligation to act according to one’s principles. That attitude to the world is reflected in the title of one of the books we worked on together: Moralpolitik. Despite this, or more likely precisely because of it, he was never the most influential or the best-known politician in Europe. But he helped me, and probably others who knew him, to keep faith in a kind of politics different from the one that prevails in the world around us today.

Besides people and Chinese food, Russell loved postcards. He wrote at least ten of them every day. One of the last ones I received came from hospital, shortly after he had learned that his illness was incurable. He mentioned that only in passing; what amused him most was that, because of the extremely strong medication, his doctors had forbidden him unprotected sex. Soon afterwards, the day before his seventy-sixth birthday, he collapsed on a street in Paris and died in a city he loved dearly. It was good to have known him, because they don’t make them like that any more, it seems to me. I sometimes wonder what Russell would say today to his colleagues, including those from his own political family who are ready to trade their values for power very quickly and very cheaply. If you like, I will gladly share those thoughts with you. Just let me be honest.
